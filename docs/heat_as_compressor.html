Heat as Compressor

Cooling is not "making cold" — it is moving heat from where you don't want it to somewhere you can dump it, and moving it uphill costs a power input. In every fridge and air conditioner that input is electricity driving a compressor. This project replaces the compressor with a bucket of adsorbent: heat itself does the pumping. The coldest, most useless grade of energy (60–120 °C waste heat) is converted into the most valuable one on a hot day (7–18 °C cooling).

You cannot run a turbine or charge a battery from 60 °C water. You can run an adsorption chiller — because its only job is to push refrigerant vapour from one pressure to another, and heat can do that directly.

1 · Water's secret — the boiling-point dial

Everything rests on one fact you have felt but maybe never used: the boiling point is set by pressure, not by a stove. Drop the pressure and water boils colder. Drop it far enough and it boils below room temperature — water boiling at 16 °C absorbs 2.47 MJ of heat per kilogram while it evaporates. That heat theft is the cooling.

pressure 1.82 kPa
Anchors: 1.0 kPa → 7 °C (vehicle evaporator) · 1.8 kPa → 16 °C (datacenter / cpu evaporator) · 5.6 kPa → 35 °C (condenser back-pressure) · atmosphere → 100 °C. The refrigerant is plain water; the trick is keeping the machine at 1/56th of atmospheric pressure.

2 · The machine you replace — electric compression

A normal fridge pumps heat with the same four stations, but the pressure step is bought with electricity: the compressor squeezes vapour from low to high pressure. That is the only place money enters the loop.

One loop, one power input. Replace the red box with something else that raises vapour pressure — and the electricity bill disappears.

3 · How heat does the squeezing — piston vs bed

Both machines must perform the identical single job: lift refrigerant vapour from the evaporator's 1.8 kPa to the condenser's 5.6 kPa — a 3.1× pressure ratio. Everything else (evaporator, condenser, load) is the same in both. The only question is what provides the lift.

Same duty, different mechanism. Left: a motor squeezes the vapour's volume — the refrigerant passes straight through. Right: the bed's equilibrium vapour pressure is a function of temperature, so a temperature swing does the "squeezing" — but the vapour must first stick to the solid, which makes the bed batch-wise (hence two beds).

The thermometer is the piston. A full MIL-100 bed (0.212 kg water per kg adsorbent) has an equilibrium pressure of exactly 1.82 kPa at 35 °C — it is the suction line. Heat that same full bed and its pressure climbs the isostere: 3.21 kPa at 45 °C, 7.04 kPa at 60 °C — 3.9× from +25 K, comfortably past the 3.1× the condenser demands. Clausius–Clapeyron sets the slope of that climb; the D–A isotherm sets where it starts. Both live in harness/physics/thermo.py.

But a piston only has to squeeze — a bed has to squeeze and let go. Two duties:

① Reach discharge pressure. Thermodynamics — any bed manages it given enough ΔT. Even zeolite 13X, the strongest binder in the table, pushes 7.0 kPa at 60 °C: duty ① passed.

② Deliver the charge. 13X's empty state (0.243 kg/kg) sits almost on top of its full state (0.246) — its two isosteres nearly coincide, so its "stroke" is ~1 % of its displacement: it discharges a whisper of vapour and quits. MIL-100's isosteres are far apart (0.212 → 0.148): it delivers 30 % of its adsorbed charge every pass. Duty ② is decided by the material — which is why the cycle map's rectangle width (§5) and the working-window gap (§7) are the real judges, and why a pressure-ratio-only view would have ranked 13X as fine.

4 · Heat as compressor — the adsorption machine, live

The replacement is a porous solid that grips water — a zeolite, a silica gel, a MOF. Gripping is easy; the art is making it let go. That is what heat does:

Cold bed + low pressure → water sticks. The bed holds the evaporator at 1.8 kPa, so the load's water keeps boiling at 16 °C and stealing heat. Adsorption also releases its own heat (the isosteric heat Qst), so the bed must be cooled while it works.

Hot bed + high pressure → water leaves. Heat the bed to 60 °C and the grip fails; vapour is driven out at the condenser's pressure — 3× the evaporator's. The bed has just compressed the refrigerant using heat, no moving parts. Two beds run counter-phase, so one is always adsorbing while the other regenerates: cooling never stops.

5 · The cycle map — how adsorption researchers draw it

The same six steps on a pressure–temperature map. The green curve is water's boiling line (psat); the dashed lines are isosteres — paths of constant water held in the bed. The bed travels the rectangle once per half-cycle. This is the "D–A operating window" panel on every application page in docs/apps — now you can read it.

Corners: 1 end of adsorption (35 °C, full) · 2 isosteric heat-up until the bed's own vapour pressure reaches p_cond · 3 end of desorption (60 °C, empty) · 4 isosteric cool-down until the bed can pull from the evaporator again. 6 · The energy ledger — where every joule goes

Cooling out is Δq·h_fg. Heat in is what you pay: heating the bed's own thermal mass up to the regeneration temperature (sensible), plus prising the water off (Δq·Qst), plus a 1.35× heat-exchanger penalty. 7 · Why the material decides — the working window

The bed holds water along two curves — how much it can hold at the adsorption pressure (blue) and how much it is still holding at the regeneration temperature under the condenser pressure (red). The cooling comes from the gap between them. Same equations, same profile — switch material and watch the gap close.

The lesson in one parameter: the characteristic energy E. 13X grips water with E ≈ 17.9 kJ/mol — magnificent for drying gas, catastrophic here: at 60 °C the bed barely lets go, Δq collapses, the cycle dead-ends (COP 0.06). MIL-100 (E ≈ 3.7 kJ/mol) releases on cue — that is why a weaker binder wins when your heat is only 60 °C. Your vehicle profile (120 °C) sits at the other edge: nothing in the current fit library was measured that hot, so every ranking row there carries the honest out_of_window flag — a measurement gap, not a winner list.

9 · What heat-as-compressor offers

The pitch in one sentence: a datacenter already pumps 45–70 °C coolant to protect its chips; your harness shows a MIL-100 bed converts that same loop into COP 0.57 cooling — the building's own waste heat pays the air-conditioning bill.

  • Free energy, right season. Waste heat peaks exactly when cooling demand peaks; no grid draw, no compressor electricity.
  • Nothing exotic inside. Water refrigerant, solid adsorbent, no moving parts in the vapour loop — the engineering risk concentrates in heat exchangers and in choosing the material, which is precisely what the ranking layer does.
  • The knobs are quantified. Source temperature (the profile), material (E, q_sat, Q_st), and schedule (H2.2: +17 % COP) each have a measured effect in the harness — so any real design conversation can start from numbers instead of vibes.
  • Honest limits, on the table. All numbers here are equilibrium; transport losses come next (PINN stage), nothing is yet measured at 120 °C (vehicle), and no hardware exists — this is the lab, not the rig.
